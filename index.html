Comparison with A Data-Driven Analysis–Resynthesis Approach    [Go to top]

In this section, we present a qualitative comparison with a data-driven analysis–resynthesis approach, where multiple aspects of a signal are analyzed, modified, and then resynthesized. While this is a common strategy in voice conversion systems, it becomes extremely challenging for complex audio signals, which require disentangling elements such as pitch, instrumentation, lyrics, and performer identity, leading to the accumulation of errors and inaccuracies. In contrast, our approach performs transformations directly within a reconstructible latent space, preserving the underlying structure and rich information of the original signal. As can be heard in the first two songs, content is somehwat captured by analysis–resynthesis, but the information loss is substantial. In the third song, analysis–resynthesis severely degrades the content, rendering it largely unrecognizable, whereas our method preserves the content while modifying pitch.

The Effect of Cycle-Consistency Fine-Tuning    [Go to top]

In this section, we present qualitative samples demonstrating the effect of our proposed cycle-consistency fine-tuning without requiring paired audio data. We apply learned pitch transformations to audio embeddings and compare decoding using the fine-tuned decoder with decoding using the original decoder. While the underlying signal structure and content are already captured without fine-tuning, the resulting audio is notably noisy, as the model was not trained on transformed embeddings. Cycle-consistency fine-tuning substantially improves audio quality, without the need for paired pitch-shifted audio data.

Comparison with Signal-Processing-Based Frequency Scaling    [Go to top]

In this section, we present qualitative samples comparing our proposed data-driven pitch-shifting method with a signal-processing-based approach using frequency scaling. As a baseline, we use the Rubber Band library, which is known for producing high-quality audio. However, as can be heard in the samples, despite its overall quality, it introduces significant timbre distortion for larger pitch shifts (e.g., three semitones or more). This effect is particularly noticeable in singing, where upward shifts can result in a “chipmunk” effect and downward shifts in a “Darth Vader” effect. These artifacts arise because frequency scaling uniformly shifts all frequencies, altering formants in a way that does not accurately reflect real-world behavior. In contrast, our data-driven approach better preserves timbre and formants, especially for larger shifts, as it learns the relationship between pitch and timbre directly from data.
